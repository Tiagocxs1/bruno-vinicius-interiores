Colour as a Tool: Building a Palette That Works

Forget matching the sofa to the rug. Colour works as a system — light, depth, and a single strong move. How to build a palette that holds together.

Most people choose colour the way they choose an outfit they love in a photo — and then wonder why the result doesn’t hold together. Colour in a room works as a system, with a hierarchy, and the system matters more than any single colour.

Start with the room’s light, not the trend

The same paint reads differently under north-facing light, west sun, and warm bulbs. A north-facing room needs warm tones to feel alive; a sun-drenched room can carry cooler ones. Before sampling colours, notice the light: what direction it comes from, how it changes through the day, and whether the bulbs are warm or cool.

The 60-30-10 structure

  • 60% — the dominant tone: walls, floor, and the largest surfaces. Keep it quiet.
  • 30% — the secondary tone: the sofa, the cabinetry, the major furniture. In conversation with the first.
  • 10% — the accent: cushions, a chair, a rug, art. This is where personality lives, and it should stay a minority shareholder.
A palette is not a collection of favourite colours. It is a set of colours that have agreed to work together.

One strong move beats five nice ones

Rooms that feel indecisive usually have several colours each doing a small job. Rooms that feel designed have one strong move — a deep wall in a small room, a saturated kitchen island, a generous coloured rug — and let everything else fall quietly around it. Decide where you want the eye to land, and put the boldest colour there and nowhere else.

A practical test

Sample on the wall, in the actual room, and look at it at three times of day. Cheap sample pots and a week of patience save the cost of a full repaint. And when you have narrowed to two palettes, choose the one that feels calmer — colour is easiest to add later and hardest to subtract.
